Canadian identity and the 1972 Summit Series

Explore the timeline ↓
CANUSSR

Introduction

On September 2, 1972, Team Canada scored two goals in the opening minutes of its first game against the Soviet Union. The Soviets won 7–3. Less than four weeks later, Paul Henderson scored with 34 seconds left in the eighth game to give Canada the series. These two moments belong to the same story, but they tell very different versions of what happened when Canada faced a strong opponent in a sport many Canadians regarded as their own. [1–2]

How did the Summit Series become a symbol of Canadian identity? What is missing when the whole series is remembered through a single goal? This exhibit explores these questions through eight records from the series. The early games of the series test the notion of Canadian hockey superiority. Television allowed Canadians to experience the games together, but the nation was split on what the team owed its fans and what fans owed the team. Today, museums and other national institutions have extended the life of certain moments from the series. The timeline in the exhibit separates the games from their subsequent commemorations, so viewers can see when a record was created and what aspects of the event it emphasizes. [1–3, 8–10]

The Montréal Opener

2 September 1972 · Game record · International Ice Hockey Federation

In the first four games of the series, played in Canada, the Soviet Union surprised observers of the Canadian game. Canada built up a two goal lead in Montreal but the Soviets stormed back to win 7-3. Canada won 4-1 in Toronto and tied 4-4 in Winnipeg. These games help set the scene for the rest of the series. [1–2]

The outcome of the first game also affects our perception of the Canadian team at the time. All of the Canadian team members were current or former NHL players, accustomed to a style of hockey that differed greatly from that played by the Soviet Union in this series. [2, 4]

Spectators and media

A Ticket to the Series

September 1972

The three items displayed in the Canadian Museum of History’s display cases are a game ticket, a souvenir banner, and a hockey puck. A ticket to a hockey game is of little value to a person after a game has been played. However, the location where the item was found can be a memento for the person who kept it. These three items, therefore, serve as mementos and as examples of how museums display items relating to events of the past to the public. [3–4]

The series was experienced by people in many different circumstances. Some saw the games in person in arenas while others followed the games on TV or radio. Parks Canada’s account of the last game of the series includes scenes of schools and workplaces opening their doors to let people watch the game, as well as fans sending telegrams to the team in Moscow and traveling to the games. A ticket to a game is one way of participating in an event. It can be easily displayed in a museum as evidence of participation by the person who collected it. For the much larger broadcast audience, memories of the series may be preserved in family stories or on tapes of the games. How can the participation of visitors to a digital archive be represented when some ways of experiencing an event leave collectibles while others leave few physical traces? [2–4]

Spectators and media

Phil Esposito Speaks After Vancouver

8 September 1972 · Postgame television interview · Phil Esposito

Fans of Team Canada had booed their team during the game in Vancouver. Phil Esposito did a television interview after the game expressing his dismay at the fans’ behavior and giving his first hand account of what had transpired on the ice. Viewers across the country got a first hand look at how frustrated the players’ fans were with their team’s play. Fans of the team felt that the players were doing a poor job and that they had the right to boo the players. Esposito, in his interview, was critical of fans who booed the players and asked viewers to put themselves in the players’ shoes. Televised hockey games had created a nationwide debate. [2, 5–6]

National pride can take many forms, including arguments about how to support the players of Team Canada. This is a look at a moment in time, when fans, players and others were trying to come to terms with the games of Team Canada. Esposito’s later recollection adds another dimension to the story. The anniversary interview provides a look back at the events of that time, with knowledge of the comeback and the fame of the speech. Putting the two accounts together provides insight into how a participant viewed the events in the middle of the series and how those events became part of the remembered story of the series. [5–6]

Yakushev’s Helmet

September 1972 · Game equipment · Hockey Hall of Fame

Alexander Yakushev’s hockey helmet was displayed by the Hall of Fame to represent the 1972 Series against Canada. According to the Hall of Fame, Yakushev was the top scorer for the Soviet team during the series. Though the Soviet players may have appeared negatively to Canadians during this series, in reality they were very skilled players and should not be dismissed. Read in conjunction with the first goal scored by Phil Esposito, the helmet gives additional insight into the Soviets’ control of the game and their ability to set the pace of play. [1–2, 7]

Comparing the equipment of the two teams gives visitors to the Hall of Fame a basis for judgment. Both teams used equipment to prepare for the same sport, but in different ways. The entry states that the helmet was displayed in an exhibit. It doesn’t explain what if anything Yakushev understood about the games. Soviet spectators may have also interpreted the games differently from Canadians. The majority of the documents in this collection were produced or filed in Canadian institutions. Therefore, to be comprehensive, an account of these games should include materials produced in the USSR. [4, 7]

Spectators and media

Frank Lennon’s Photograph

28 September 1972 · Archival press photograph · Frank Lennon

The final game of the series was entered by Canada in the third period, down 5–3. Goals by Phil Esposito and Yvan Cournoyer tied the game before Henderson’s goal in the third period won the game for Canada 6–5. This moment of the winning goal was captured by Toronto Star photographer Frank Lennon and is reproduced by the Canadian Museum of History with the Library and Archives Canada reference number. The image conveys the immediate reaction to the goal but does not include the earlier losses, the team’s rallies, and the relentless pressure from the Soviets throughout the series. [1–2, 8]

The single image can be understood as a visual short story, already known to the viewer. It is much easier to remember and to reproduce this single moment, to celebrate it, than to remember a whole month of games. In order to understand the single goal, Henderson scored in the final game of the series, the record of the game itself is needed. This record conveys the sequence of goals scored by both teams and the margin of victory, which Lennon’s image cannot convey. It also conveys the emotional release of the goal, which Lennon’s image cannot convey. Moreover, the comparison of the two records reveals the role of other players, such as Cournoyer, in a story that is usually told using Henderson’s name alone. [1–2, 8]

Henderson’s Moscow Jersey

September 1972 · Game jersey documented by the Canadian Museum of History

The Canadian Museum of History identifies this jersey as one Henderson wore in Moscow and credits the private collection of Mitchell Goldhar. The jersey connects a named player to the competition through a surviving object that a later visitor can inspect. Its maple leaf makes the team’s national role visible. It also carries a connection to an individual athlete. Those two meanings meet whenever a museum uses one player’s jersey to help explain why the games mattered to a much larger public. [4, 9]

Displayed after a famous victory, the jersey can make its wearer look like the inevitable centre of the story. During the series that position was still being made. Canada lost in Moscow before winning the final three games, and Henderson scored the winning goal in each of those wins. The game records show why the jersey became especially valuable as a historical object. They also show how much of the event would disappear if the display focused on it alone. The blue line, goaltending, opposition scoring, and the play of Henderson’s teammates all belong in the competitive story, even when the museum object gives visitors a single player as an entry point. [1–2, 9]

Public memory

The Canada Post Anniversary Stamp

21 September 2022 · Commemorative stamp · Canada Post

The anniversary stamp features an image of a faceoff between Phil Esposito and a Soviet player in game 3 of the series. The image, created by designer Jim Ryce and illustrator Gary Alphonso, is set in a TV frame. The stamp, and the memories it can bring back, are featured on the Canada Post website, and are being used to ask Canadians where they were during the final game of the series. [10]

The television frame symbolizes that the Canadian public watched the games together, some 50 years ago. Today, the outline of that equipment is a symbol of having experienced the games together. This stamp can also be used by a generation that never saw the games. A person buying this stamp 50 years later would learn about the games through the design of the stamp or through the stories told by a relative of that person. Commemorative objects can reproduce a sense of shared memory long after the original audience has scattered. [2, 10]

Public memory

The Royal Canadian Mint Two Dollar Coin

2022 · Commemorative circulation coin · Royal Canadian Mint

The Royal Canadian Mint’s anniversary coin gives the series a place in everyday circulation. Now people have the opportunity to add a small piece of this history to their purse or pocket. A maple leaf background frames Canadian players. Bilingual inscriptions on the coin name the series and acknowledge the 50th anniversary. Compared to the stamp design, this coin places the players closer to the national emblem. Also, using two languages on the coin reflects how audiences could have chosen to listen to the final game in French or in English. [2, 11]

The coin and stamp both mark the 50th anniversary. Their designs make different choices about what part of the event to carry forward. The coin offers a national team people can recognize through its name, uniforms, and emblem. The stamp recalls the audience’s experience of gathering around a screen. Neither format can show the uncertainty of the early games as fully as a timeline can. The objects are strongest as evidence of what institutions chose to emphasize in 2022, when the outcome was already secure and the greater challenge was explaining why any of it still mattered. [10–11]

Comparing the records

The early scorecards and the interview with Paul Henderson in Vancouver demonstrate how camaraderie can develop from disagreements. Fans watched the games in the same way, but the Montréal loss and the booing of Team Canada in Vancouver indicate that being part of a public event does not mean agreeing on the purpose of the event. The broadcast allowed a player to request more support before the same audience that had just seen his team lose. Fifty years later, the stamp represents the television audience as a shared national memory. Placing the records side-by-side reveals something the anniversary design smoothed over. Watching together can include awkwardness, criticism, and competing ideas of loyalty. [1–2, 5–6, 10]

The equipment used by the players also limits the extent to which the series is a complete portrait of the country. Athletes were selected to win a men's elite competition. Players could represent Canada publicly while representing only one aspect of Canadian hockey. Hockey Canada states that the first IIHF-sanctioned women's world championship was in 1990. The difference shows how unevenly visible were opportunities for international competition. This archive includes more information about Canadian players and institutions than it does about Soviet spectators or players. A single event may have reached many people; however, the remaining collections make some participants much easier to find than others. [4, 7, 9, 12]

Conclusion

The Summit Series became more than a set of hockey games because people watched a national team fail to meet expectations, argued about what it meant to support that team, and later gave the comeback a visible place in Canadian public memory. The scores, objects, and media records let us follow that process at different points in time. Together they show that a sense of national belonging can come from taking part in a shared event and debating its meaning. The archive preserves the excitement of Henderson’s goal while making room for the setbacks, opposition, and institutional choices that helped give it such a long afterlife.
